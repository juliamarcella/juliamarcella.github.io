01Customer Support · ChurnNew

Customer Support Performance & Churn

"What makes customers unhappy, and can support tickets warn us before they leave?"

GitHub Slides
What I did

I worked in customer support for almost four years, so I wanted a project about the job I know from the inside. I generated two years of helpdesk data for a made-up software company, Flowbase: 17,000+ tickets from 3,200 customers, with a platform outage and a price increase hidden in it. Then I tested whether my analysis could find them.

What I found
  • Speed drives satisfaction. When a ticket missed the SLA, 28.9% of customers gave a low score, against 5.5% when it was on time.
  • The most urgent tickets wait the longest. 34.2% of Urgent tickets missed the SLA, about 3× the average.
  • CSAT can hide churn. After the price increase, Billing tickets almost doubled and churn within 90 days rose from 8.6% to 13.9%, while CSAT stayed good.
  • Churn is partly predictable. A logistic regression flags 61.7% of the customers who leave (ROC-AUC 0.70).

The problem

Most customers who cancel never give a reason, so exit surveys don't explain churn. Support tickets are one of the few early signals a company already collects every day.

The approach

Data generation in Python, cleaning where every missing value was explained instead of filled, EDA on speed, CSAT and churn, before/after checks for the two business events, and a logistic regression trained without leakage columns.

What a business can do

Answer Urgent and Performance tickets first, especially during incidents. Treat Billing tickets after a price change as an early churn warning and pass them to Customer Success. Use the model to build a check-in list.

Honest limits: the data is synthetic, so patterns are cleaner than in real life. Only 39.5% of tickets have a CSAT score, and before/after comparisons show a link, not proof of cause.

02E-Commerce · Customer Analytics

Customer Segmentation & Lifetime Value

"Who are the most valuable customers, and what will they spend next?"

GitHub Power BI Dashboard
What I did

A UK online retailer had 375K transactions but treated every customer the same. I built RFM metrics, used K-Means to group customers, trained a Random Forest to predict future spend, and put it all in a four-page Power BI dashboard with DAX measures.

What I found
  • Three very different customer types. A tiny VIP group, a large core group (72.87%) and an at-risk group (26.98%) that hadn't bought in 200+ days.
  • Volume isn't revenue. The best seller by units is not the top earner, so promotions based on volume push the wrong products.
  • I caught my own mistake. The first CLV model had data leakage. I found it, fixed it, and corrected how average order value was calculated.

The problem

Revenue data, but no customer intelligence: no segments and no way to tell a loyal customer from a one-time buyer, so no basis for where to spend on marketing.

The approach

RFM for every customer, K-Means clustering into three behavioural segments, a Random Forest CLV model, and cohort analysis to see how value builds month by month.

What a business can do

Put retention budget on VIPs first, re-engage at-risk customers before they go quiet, and plan campaigns on revenue contribution instead of units sold.

03Health · Workplace

Menstrual Cycle & Workplace Productivity

"Does pain affect how much work someone can do, and can we predict it?"

GitHub Dashboard Slides
What I did

I analysed 17,972 cycle logs from 2,000 users, comparing symptoms and lifestyle (sleep, exercise, diet, stress) with hours of work lost, mood and energy. Then I built a Random Forest model to estimate productivity loss from symptoms, and a five-page Looker Studio dashboard.

What I found
  • Pain is the main driver. It explained more of the lost hours than cycle phase, diet or lifestyle.
  • It's the majority, not a niche. Over half of users report PMS symptoms, so policies that ignore it miss most people.
  • Targeted beats generic. Flexible options on high-pain days help more than general wellness programmes.

04Sport · Gender · Storytelling

Women in the Olympic Games

"How long did women have to wait, and where does inequality still hide?"

GitHub Dashboard
What I did

Using 163,000+ athlete records from 1896 to 2022, I created a "gender entry gap" for every sport: the years between the first men's and first women's event. I tracked participation by decade, mapped medals by gender and country, and built an interactive dashboard with filters.

What I found
  • Progress is real. Women's participation grew sharply from the 1980s, as new women's events were added.
  • But some sports waited over a century. Rugby took 116 years, wrestling 108.
  • Medals follow events. More women's events directly means more medals for women.
